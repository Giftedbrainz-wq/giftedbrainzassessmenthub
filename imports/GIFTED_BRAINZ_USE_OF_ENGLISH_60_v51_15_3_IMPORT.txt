It may be difficult to prove an exact correlation between the viewer of fantasized violence and the criminal who acts out violence after turning off the set. But if the premise of education is granted – that good models can influence the young – then it follows that bad models can have an equivalent harmful effect. Witness enough mimed shootouts, see enough ‘corpses’ fall across the screen, and the taking of a human life seems no big deal. Even if a simple causal relationship cannot be established between watching violence and acting it out, is not this numbed sensitivity reason enough for cutting back on the overkill in films and TV?

EXPLANATIONS
1. Repeated exposure blunts the ability to respond emotionally to violence.
2. The author extends the premise about learning from good models to the potential harmful influence of violent ones.
3. The figures concern victims and the risk of being killed. They give no information about who fired.
4. The phrase compares firearm deaths over time. The passage still places road deaths first.
5. Alarming and unbearable correspond to the Secretary’s judgement that the figures were frightening and intolerable.
6. The Senate is a legislative body. The Senate is part of the legislative branch of government, concerned principally with lawmaking. “Executive” refers to implementing government policy, while “judicial” relates to the courts.
7. To nullify an action is to render it invalid. To nullify an action is to make it legally ineffective or invalid. The speaker questions whether the Senate can cancel a presidential act; enforcing or merely modifying it would mean something different.
8. An arm of an institution is one of its constituent parts. An “arm of” an institution means a constituent branch or part of it. The Senate is one chamber of Nigeria’s National Assembly, so the figurative noun “arm” is appropriate in this formal context.
9. The House of Representatives forms the other chamber of the National Assembly. The House of Representatives is the other chamber of the Nigerian National Assembly. The writer warns that its view could conflict with the Senate’s, which makes this institutional name the correct completion.
10. A plaintiff brings a case before a court. A plaintiff is the party who brings a legal action and makes an allegation for the court to determine. The author accuses the Senate of acting as both accuser and judge in the matter being discussed.
11. Legislatures enact laws. To enact laws is to pass them through the recognised legislative process. “Create” is too general and “annul” means cancel rather than make law.
12. Power refers to legal authority to act. “Legal power” refers to the authority to perform a particular action. The writer is discussing the limits of the Senate’s constitutional authority, not its physical strength or dynamism.
13. The President exercises executive powers. “Executive powers” are powers associated with the President’s role in implementing and administering the law. The paragraph contrasts these powers with the legislature’s lawmaking function.
14. Legal powers may be regulated by legislation. “Regulated by” suggests that the exercise of powers may be governed or limited by a legislative rule. “Standardised” usually refers to uniform procedures, not the legal regulation described.
15. An Act of the National Assembly is enacted legislation. An Act is legislation that has completed the necessary lawmaking process. A bill is only proposed legislation, whereas a motion or resolution is not, by itself, an Act of the National Assembly.
16. The narrator connects Bepo’s inaction to his reluctance to relocate.
17. The passport was already out of date before the pandemic disrupted renewal.
18. The celebration honours the contribution he made to Stardom over time.
19. The public tribute celebrates a relationship whose possible ending is painful to him.
20. The monarch receives the group and explains the town’s origins and trade.
21. His examples broaden the students’ understanding of the town’s economic past.
22. The visit ties the history of enslavement to the place from which captives were taken away.
23. The Badagry visit lets pupils question a local authority and examine sites associated with their lessons.
24. The translation shown to the pupils connects the building with written Yoruba and Christian missionary history.
25. Professional belonging and family ties pull the principal in different directions.
26. The hypothetical condition implies his absence made the occasion less enjoyable.
27. A jaundiced judgement is prejudiced or unfavourable.
28. In this context only restricts the gift to the card.
29. No love lost expresses mutual dislike.
30. Inflammatory language is provocative and can stir anger.
31. A fleeting visit is brief; a long one is its opposite.
32. Alleviate means ease; exacerbate means make worse.
33. Detriment is harm, whereas advantage is a benefit.
34. Brakes that act up malfunction. Brakes responding well do the opposite.
35. Seeing eye to eye means agreeing.
36. To browbeat is to intimidate or bully.
37. A nebulous topic is indistinct or vague.
38. Here pleasantries are light, friendly or jocular remarks.
39. The figurative apogee is the highest point of an achievement.
40. Respite is a temporary period of relief.
41. The modal perfect requires might + have + past participle.
42. Since morning marks a period continuing to the present, calling for the present perfect.
43. Drought-resistant describes trees able to withstand dry conditions.
44. Offhand here means without preparation or reference.
45. A turncoat abandons one political allegiance for another.
46. To buy up goods is to purchase all or a large available supply.
47. From the outset means from the beginning.
48. Swift-footed describes those able to run quickly.
49. Assent is formal approval of a bill.
50. Equipment is an uncountable noun and can take enough without a plural ending.
51. Boat and go share /əʊ/ in standard British pronunciation.
52. Air and heir are homophones.
53. Here and the noun tear share the same stressed vowel and ending.
54. Theatre and thought begin with the voiceless dental fricative /θ/.
